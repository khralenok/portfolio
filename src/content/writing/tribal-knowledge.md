---
title: "Tribal Knowledge Trap"
description: "Why shared product understanding matters, and how to make it accessible to the whole team."
---

Have you ever scrolled through a landing page and still had no idea what the company actually does? You are not alone. I have built some of those pages myself. The prime suspect is often the Curse of Knowledge. Immersed in the product, we overlook essential details, assuming our audience already knows what we know. Yet the real culprit tends to be Tribal Knowledge. When understanding varies from person to person, the landing page becomes a messy reflection of the team's internal confusion.

Diagnosing this bias is straightforward. The clearest symptom is the absence of a documented product knowledge base. To clarify, I am not referring to API documentation, sales scripts, target audience profiles, vertical descriptions, or pitch decks. Those serve specific functions. A true knowledge base is a dedicated body of documents with one purpose: to educate the reader about the product itself. 

It must be written in role-agnostic language. Whether the reader is a developer, salesperson, or designer, their understanding should be consistent. It should function as a single source of truth—a product "bible" the team can quote with confidence. 

People in large organizations might object that they already have a knowledge base—one that is practically unreadable. That is a fair point. Large companies manage complex products, and no one can absorb everything. In such environments, knowledge is often segmented to maintain momentum. In small teams, however, the situation is different. To build effectively, every member needs a coherent mental model of the entire product.

A confusing landing page is merely the tip of the iceberg. The deeper consequences include prolonged onboarding, inefficient outsourcing, and an invisible growth ceiling that limits scalability. Imagine every new hire or contractor having to interview the entire team just to assemble a fragmented understanding of the product—one that may still be inaccurate.

So how do you build a knowledge base? 

Start with a rough draft—perfection is unnecessary. A plain text file is enough.

Feed it into an AI chatbot and ask it to structure the information into a coherent knowledge base, then export it as a Markdown file.

Import it into Notion, Confluence, or any tool your team uses.

Schedule a monthly review call to refine the document and identify outdated or unclear sections.

From there, assign clarification tasks as needed. Over time, the knowledge base will become both comprehensive and clear. It requires ongoing maintenance, but a well-structured knowledge base invariably pays for itself.
